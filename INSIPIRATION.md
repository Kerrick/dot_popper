# Inspiration

This is optional background reading for the presentation and game-feel work: key ideas from books on interaction design, visual design, game design and writing. Nothing here is a requirement. Where it conflicts with the spec, the spec wins.

These summaries were written from an AI model's memory, not checked against the books. Treat them as starting points, not as quotes or citations. Summaries marked "less certain" deserve extra skepticism.

---

## Part 1: Interaction, UX and visual design

### Interaction design and human factors

**Interaction Design: Beyond Human-Computer Interaction (Rogers, Sharp & Preece)**
- Design is an iterative process: discover needs, design alternatives, prototype, and evaluate, then repeat.
- Involve users from the beginning, and set usability goals and user-experience goals separately.
- Base your designs on what's known about how people think and feel, including cognition, emotion and social behavior.
- Choose research and evaluation methods that fit the question you're trying to answer: interviews, observation, usability testing, field studies or analytics.

**Designing for People (Lee, Wickens, Liu & Boyle)**
This is the human factors engineering textbook. Don't confuse it with Henry Dreyfuss's 1955 book of the same name.
- Design systems to fit what people can actually do, rather than training people to fit the system.
- Perception, attention, memory and decision-making all have known limits that can be measured, so design within them.
- Most "human error" is really a design problem. Prevent errors by making the right action obvious and the wrong one hard.
- Trust in automation should match how reliable the automation really is. People who trust it too much or too little both cause failures.

**Designing with the Mind in Mind (Jeff Johnson)**
- What we see is shaped by what we expect to see, so users often miss things they didn't expect.
- Gestalt principles (proximity, similarity, enclosure) create structure before anyone reads a word.
- Attention and working memory are very limited. Recognizing an option is far easier than recalling it.
- Reading is a learned skill, not a natural one, so poor text design slows everyone down.
- Response time matters in specific ranges, because each range of delay feels different to the user.
- Design guidelines work because of this underlying psychology, so knowing why a guideline works lets you apply it wisely.

**Microinteractions (Dan Saffer)**
- Every small interaction has four parts: a trigger, the rules, the feedback, and its loops and modes.
- Small details decide whether a product feels polished. As the book puts it, "the details are the design."
- Don't make users start from nothing. Use what you already know about them.
- Show the most important information right in the trigger itself. Saffer calls this "bring the data forward."
- Feedback should fit the moment and never be more than it needs to be.
- Avoid modes when you can, and make a mode obvious when you can't.

**Designing Interactions (Bill Moggridge)**
- Interviews with pioneers behind the mouse, the desktop interface, the PalmPilot, IDEO and more.
- Moggridge co-coined the term "interaction design," and the book traces how the field grew up.
- Its recurring lesson is to prototype early and often, because people understand an interaction by experiencing it, not by reading a specification.
- Good interaction design brings together technology, business and an understanding of people.

**Make It So (Shedroff & Noessel)**
- Science fiction interfaces are a lab for interaction design, and they shape what audiences expect from real technology.
- It covers controls, visual interfaces, volumetric projections, gestures, sound, brain interfaces, augmented reality, anthropomorphism and more.
- It draws many short lessons from these, such as: build on what users already know, gestures need a vocabulary people can learn, and anthropomorphism raises expectations an interface has to live up to.
- Interfaces made to look impressive on screen often perform badly for real use. Comparing the two teaches a lot about both.

### Experience and narrative

**Playful Design (John Ferrara)**
- Game design is a discipline UX can learn from, not decoration to add on top of a product.
- Adding points and badges is shallow. A playful experience needs meaningful goals, challenge, feedback and choices that matter to the player.
- Motivation and fun come from how a system is structured, not from surface rewards.
- Games can persuade and teach by letting players try out a system for themselves.

**Storytelling for User Experience (Quesenbery & Brooks)**
- Stories are tools at every stage of design: collecting research, understanding it, sharing it, and imagining designs.
- A good user story has a specific person, context, goal and conflict.
- Tailor each story to the audience it's meant to convince.
- Personas and scenarios work best as narratives, not as lists of attributes.

### The "Universal" reference series (Rockport)

**Universal Principles of Design (Lidwell, Holden & Butler)**
- An A–Z encyclopedia of principles drawn from many fields: the 80/20 rule, affordance, aesthetic-usability effect, Fitts's law, Hick's law, progressive disclosure, mental models, MAYA ("most advanced yet acceptable"), and more.
- Principles apply across media, from architecture to interfaces.
- Each one is a heuristic to understand before you apply it, not a law.

**Universal Methods of Design (Martin & Hanington)**
- A catalog of about 100 research and design methods: diary studies, card sorting, journey maps, Wizard-of-Oz prototyping and more.
- Pick a method based on the phase of your project and the question you're asking.
- Combine qualitative and quantitative methods, and combine what people say with what they do.

**Universal Principles of UX (Irene Pereyra)** *(less certain)*
- About 100 principles covering research, strategy, information architecture, interaction and the design process.
- It treats UX as a practice that understands people first, rather than as a list of interface patterns.

**Universal Principles of Color (Westland & Maggio)** *(less certain)*
- Color is something we perceive, not something objects have, so context, lighting and nearby colors change how a color looks.
- Color harmony, contrast and color models are tools to use deliberately.
- Color meanings depend on culture, and color is never a reliable way to carry information by itself, since many people see color differently.

**Universal Principles of Typography (Elliot Jay Stocks)**
- Legibility (telling letters apart) and readability (reading comfortably) are two different goals.
- Choose typefaces for their purpose and setting before choosing for style.
- Hierarchy, line length, line spacing and letter spacing decide whether text gets read.
- Pair typefaces sparingly and for a reason, and take screen typography into account.

### Graphic design and type

**Thinking with Type (Ellen Lupton)**
- The book is organized into three parts: Letter, Text and Grid.
- Understanding the anatomy and history of type helps you make better choices.
- Spacing (kerning, tracking, leading) and alignment matter as much as which typeface you pick.
- Typography is an interface: it helps readers navigate, scan and understand.

**The Elements of Graphic Design (Alex W. White)**
- White space is an active design element, not leftover emptiness.
- Unity, hierarchy and clear entry points help readers move through a page in the right order.
- Treat type as a visual shape on the page, not just as words.
- Every element should have a reason to be where it is.

### Themes that run through all of them
- Design for the actual human. Perception, attention and memory have limits that you can predict.
- Iterate and prototype. Understanding comes from testing and trying, not from specs.
- Details and structure come before decoration, whether in a microinteraction, a type hierarchy or white space.
- Meaning comes from context: in color, stories, games and culture.

## Part 2: A game design reading list

### Core game design
- **The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses (Jesse Schell):** over 100 "lenses," each a question to ask about your game. It's the closest game-design equivalent to *Universal Principles of Design*.
- **Game Design Workshop (Tracy Fullerton):** a process-focused textbook built around designing by playtesting. It parallels *Interaction Design* by Rogers, Sharp & Preece.
- **Rules of Play (Katie Salen & Eric Zimmerman):** the foundational academic framework, looking at games as rules, as play, and as culture.
- **The Game Design Reader (Salen & Zimmerman, eds.):** a companion anthology of classic essays.
- **A Theory of Fun for Game Design (Raph Koster):** a short, illustrated argument that fun is the brain learning patterns.
- **Elements of Game Design (Robert Zubek):** a concise, modern introduction built around mechanics, systems and the player experience.
- **Designing Games (Tynan Sylvester):** practical and grounded, by the designer of RimWorld. Strong on emergent behavior and the decisions players make.
- **Challenges for Game Designers (Brenda Romero & Ian Schreiber):** design exercises, many of them non-digital.
- **Fundamentals of Game Design (Ernest Adams):** a broad textbook covering genres, core mechanics and balance.

### Systems, mechanics and balance
- **Characteristics of Games (George Skaff Elias, Richard Garfield & Robert Gutschera):** frameworks for luck, skill, interaction and length.
- **Game Mechanics: Advanced Game Design (Ernest Adams & Joris Dormans):** models game economies as resource flows, using the "Machinations" diagram notation.
- **Advanced Game Design: A Systems Approach (Michael Sellers):** systems thinking applied to games.
- **Game Balance (Ian Schreiber & Brenda Romero):** cost curves, progression, probability and analytics.
- **Uncertainty in Games (Greg Costikyan):** a short book on the many sources of uncertainty in games, not just random chance. It pairs well with Garfield's views on luck.
- **Players Making Decisions (Zack Hiwiller):** game theory, probability and how designers frame decisions.

### Game feel and moment-to-moment experience
- **Game Feel (Steve Swink):** the standard book on how controls, response and polish make a game feel good. It's the direct counterpart to *Microinteractions*.
- **Talks (not books):** "Juice It or Lose It" (Martin Jonasson & Petri Purho) and "The Art of Screenshake" (Jan Willem Nijman of Vlambeer). Both are short and very practical.

### Psychology and player UX
- **The Gamer's Brain (Celia Hodent):** cognitive science and UX for games, covering perception, memory, attention, motivation and how readable a game is. It's the closest match to Johnson's *Designing with the Mind in Mind*.
- **Glued to Games (Scott Rigby & Richard Ryan):** why games motivate players, using self-determination theory (competence, autonomy and relatedness).
- **The Art of Failure (Jesper Juul):** why we enjoy games that make us fail.
- **Half-Real (Jesper Juul):** games as real rules set in fictional worlds.

### Play, meaning and persuasion
- **Play Matters (Miguel Sicart):** play as a way of engaging with the world, not just with games.
- **Games: Agency as Art (C. Thi Nguyen):** a philosophy book arguing that games are an art form whose medium is agency itself.
- **The Grasshopper (Bernard Suits):** the classic definition of a game as "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles."
- **Homo Ludens (Johan Huizinga) and Man, Play and Games (Roger Caillois):** the classic works of play theory. Caillois's four categories of play include alea, play based on chance.
- **Persuasive Games (Ian Bogost):** how game rules themselves make arguments.
- **Reality Is Broken (Jane McGonigal):** a popular, optimistic case for what games can do.

### Narrative
- **Hamlet on the Holodeck (Janet Murray):** foundational thinking about interactive narrative.
- **Video Game Storytelling (Evan Skolnick):** practical story craft for game teams.

### Level, world and space design
- **An Architectural Approach to Level Design (Christopher Totten):** spatial design principles applied to game levels.
- **Level Up! The Guide to Great Video Game Design (Scott Rogers):** an approachable, illustrated practitioner's guide.
- **Designing Virtual Worlds (Richard Bartle):** the source of Bartle's well-known player types.

### Procedural generation and roguelikes
- **Procedural Generation in Game Design (Tanya Short & Tarn Adams, eds.):** essays from practicing developers, including the co-creator of Dwarf Fortress.
- **Procedural Content Generation in Games (Shaker, Togelius & Nelson):** a more technical, academic treatment.
- **Dungeon Hacks (David L. Craddock):** a history of the roguelike, from Rogue to Diablo.
- **Spelunky (Derek Yu, Boss Fight Books):** the designer's account of making a landmark roguelite.
- **Game Design Deep Dive: Roguelikes (Joshua Bycer):** *(less certain about the details)*

### Tabletop and card game design
- **Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design (Geoffrey Engelstein & Isaac Shalev):** a catalog of board game mechanisms. Works like *Universal Methods of Design* for tabletop.
- **GameTek (Geoffrey Engelstein):** the math and psychology behind tabletop design.
- **Achievement Relocked (Geoffrey Engelstein):** loss aversion and other behavioral economics in games.
- **Kobold Guide to Board Game Design (Mike Selinker, ed.):** essays from well-known tabletop designers, Richard Garfield among them.
- **Eurogames (Stewart Woods):** the history and design culture of German-style board games.

### Craft and production
- **Game Programming Patterns (Robert Nystrom):** software patterns applied to games, free online.
- **Blood, Sweat, and Pixels (Jason Schreier):** behind-the-scenes accounts of how games actually get made.

### Writing (for rules and the interface's words)
- **Strategic Writing for UX (Torrey Podmajersky):** writing an interface's words as part of its design, with patterns for instructions, errors, buttons and messages, each fitted to the state the person is in.
- **Microcopy: The Complete Guide (Kinneret Yifrah):** the short words on buttons, prompts and empty states, and how much they decide.
- **Writing Is Designing (Michael J. Metts & Andy Welfle):** words as an interface's material, written alongside the rest of the design, not poured in afterward.
- **Nicely Said (Nicole Fenton & Kate Kiefer Lee):** writing for the web in a clear, human voice.
- **Letting Go of the Words (Janice "Ginny" Redish):** writing for people who scan rather than read.
- **The Elements of Style (William Strunk Jr. & E. B. White):** the short classic of plain English, source of "omit needless words."
- **On Writing Well (William Zinsser):** clarity, simplicity and cutting clutter in nonfiction.
- **Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace (Joseph M. Williams):** why a sentence is hard to read, and how to revise it: characters as subjects, actions as verbs, old information before new.
- **The Sense of Style (Steven Pinker):** a cognitive scientist's guide to writing that a reader can follow, and to the "curse of knowledge."
- **Game Writing: Narrative Skills for Videogames (Chris Bateman, ed.):** essays on writing for games, including instructional and interface text.

For ongoing learning, the free talks in the GDC Vault are the best source, including many design postmortems.

## Part 3: Key takeaways from the most-recommended game books

### The canon (the books most often called essential)

**The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses (Jesse Schell)**
- The game is not the experience. You design the rules, but what you actually ship is the experience the player has, which you can only shape indirectly.
- Use lenses. More than 100 questions, each looking at the game from a different angle ("What does the player feel?", "Where is the challenge?"). Rotating through them keeps you from fixating on one aspect.
- Iterate quickly and cheaply. Make the cheapest prototype that answers the question at hand, test it, and repeat.
- The elemental tetrad. Every game has four parts that must support each other: mechanics, story, aesthetics and technology.
- Listen carefully. That means listening to players, to your team, to the game itself, and to yourself.

**Rules of Play (Katie Salen & Eric Zimmerman)**
- Meaningful play happens when players can see the results of their actions and those results connect to the larger game.
- Three ways to look at a game: as rules (its formal system), as play (the experience), and as culture (how it lives in the world).
- The magic circle. A game creates its own space, where arbitrary rules take on meaning.
- Second-order design. You design rules, not play directly, so you shape experiences indirectly.

**A Theory of Fun for Game Design (Raph Koster)**
- Fun is learning. The brain enjoys finding and mastering patterns.
- Boredom is mastery. Once a game's patterns are fully learned, the fun fades, so lasting games keep giving you new patterns to learn.
- Games are abstract practice for skills and situations we care about.
- Games can mature as an art form by teaching richer patterns about people and the world.

**Game Design Workshop (Tracy Fullerton)**
- Playtest from the very first day, starting with a paper prototype.
- Formal elements (players, objectives, procedures, rules, resources, conflict, boundaries and outcome) give you a checklist for what makes up any game.
- Dramatic elements (challenge, play, premise, character and story) are what get players emotionally invested.
- Design for an experience goal. Write down what you want players to feel, then test against that goal.

### The craft classics

**Game Feel (Steve Swink)**
- Game feel has three parts: real-time control, a simulated space, and polish.
- How fast the game responds matters. Below roughly 100 ms, an action feels like it happens directly. Above that, it feels like a separate step.
- Polish is not decoration. Animation, sound, screen shake and particles tell the player what happened and make it feel substantial.
- You can measure feel and tune it by adjusting things like how fast motion speeds up and slows down, and how input gets mapped to response.

**Designing Games (Tynan Sylvester)**
- Games are engines that produce experiences, and the mechanics are the parts of the engine.
- Emergence beats scripting. Simple systems that interact produce stories the designer never wrote.
- Elegance means a lot of depth from little complexity.
- Decisions are the core of play, and the most interesting ones have unclear, competing consequences.
- Iterate based on what players actually do, not on what you predict they'll do.

**Game Programming Patterns (Robert Nystrom)**
- Common design patterns, explained in game terms: command, observer, state, component, game loop, update method and more.
- Decoupling keeps systems like rules, effects and the interface from tangling together.
- The command pattern makes undo, replays and an AI opponent much easier to build.
- Learn the costs before you choose a pattern. Every pattern buys flexibility at some price.

### The thinking books

**The Art of Failure (Jesper Juul)**
- The paradox of failure: we avoid failing in real life but look for it in games.
- Failure feels fair when we can blame ourselves. If a loss feels caused by the game rather than by the player, it frustrates them.
- Kinds of failure: failing because of skill, failing because of chance, and failing because of the effort a player didn't invest. Each feels different.
- Failure is what gives success meaning. Without the possibility of losing, winning isn't satisfying.

**Games: Agency as Art (C. Thi Nguyen)**
- Games are the art form of agency. Designers create the ways a player acts: their goals, abilities and limits.
- Players take on temporary goals. We adopt a game's goals for the experience of pursuing them.
- Striving play: we often play to struggle, not just to win, the way a climber values the climb rather than just reaching the top.
- The risk of gamifying real life: simplified scoring systems can push out richer values once they're applied outside games.

**The Grasshopper (Bernard Suits)**
- A game is "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles."
- Lusory attitude: players accept the game's inefficient rules because those rules make the activity possible.
- Play can be the point of life. Suits uses a playful thought experiment to argue that playing games may be the ideal human activity.

### Most beloved by practicing designers

**The Gamer's Brain (Celia Hodent)**
- Perception is subjective. Players see what they expect to see, so test whether your intentions actually come across.
- Attention and memory are scarce. Teach new mechanics gradually, and avoid overloading the player.
- Usability and engagement are two separate goals. You need both an interface that's easy to use and real reasons to keep playing.
- Motivation, emotion and how the game flows decide whether players stay.

**Characteristics of Games (George Skaff Elias, Richard Garfield & Robert Gutschera)**
- Luck and skill aren't opposites. Both can be tuned independently.
- Games can be described by their characteristics: number of players, length, rules complexity, how much players interact, and so on. Each affects the others.
- The metagame, everything outside a single match, is designable.
- Catch-up mechanics, "kingmaking" (a player who can't win deciding who does), and reliance on memory are recurring problems with known trade-offs.

**Blood, Sweat, and Pixels (Jason Schreier)**
- Every game is a near-disaster. Shipped games survive chaos, pivots and cuts.
- Scope is the enemy. Most crises come from trying to make too much.
- Tools and pipelines matter as much as vision does.
- Iteration requires features that can be thrown away, which means being willing to cut work you love.

### The writing classics

**Strategic Writing for UX (Torrey Podmajersky)**
- The words are part of the interface's design, and the work of writing them begins with what the person is trying to do.
- Write for the state the person is in now: what has happened, and what they can do next.
- Be purposeful, concise, conversational and clear, in that order of checking.
- Each kind of text has a job and a pattern: a title orients, an instruction says what to do, a button names the action it takes.

**Microcopy: The Complete Guide (Kinneret Yifrah)**
- The smallest texts carry the most weight per word: a button, a prompt, an empty state, an error.
- Tell people what will happen, not how the system works.
- A consistent voice makes a product feel like one thing.

**The Elements of Style (William Strunk Jr. & E. B. White)**
- Omit needless words.
- Use the active voice, and put statements in positive form.
- Use definite, specific, concrete language.

**On Writing Well (William Zinsser)**
- Clutter is the disease of writing; most first drafts can be cut by half.
- Write simply, for one reader, and respect their time.
- Rewriting is where the writing gets good.

**Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace (Joseph M. Williams)**
- A sentence reads easily when its subject names the character and its verb names the action.
- Put what the reader already knows first, and what's new last, where the stress falls.
- Clarity is something you revise toward, judged by the reader, not felt by the writer.

**The Sense of Style (Steven Pinker)**
- The curse of knowledge: writers forget what readers don't know, so they skip steps and use private shorthand.
- Classic style shows the reader something, as if pointing at it, rather than describing the writer's thoughts about it.
- Coherence comes from making the connections between sentences explicit.

### Ideas these books share
1. You design the system and players experience the result, so test the experience directly (Schell, Salen & Zimmerman, Fullerton).
2. Meaningful decisions are the core of play (Sylvester, Salen & Zimmerman, Garfield).
3. Fun comes from learning and mastery, and the challenge has to be calibrated (Koster, Juul, Nguyen).
4. Prototype early and cheaply, and playtest relentlessly (Schell, Fullerton, Sylvester).
5. Feel and feedback are part of the design, not polish added later (Swink, Hodent).
